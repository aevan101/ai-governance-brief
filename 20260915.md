# Tuesday, September 15, 2026

**State AI regulation loses its fear of Silicon Valley.** Legislators are increasingly ignoring warnings from the tech lobby and its Washington allies about an unworkable "patchwork" of conflicting laws, and surging public opposition to AI is a large part of why. In Massachusetts, for example, lawmakers are [advancing an AI safety bill](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/21/openai-backs-watered-down-massachusetts-ai-safety-bill-01006369) even tougher than those approved by Illinois, California, and New York. Rhode Island [passed a law in June](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/rhode-island-enacts-four-new-ai-laws-including-a-therapy-chatbot-ban) requiring new guardrails for AI chatbots to counter the technology's psychological harms. And California legislators are [pushing for an aggressive package of bills](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/17/newsoms-silicon-valley-dilemma-comes-to-a-head-friend-or-foe-01002515), including rules on AI audits, chatbots, and data privacy. Even deep-red states like Utah and South Carolina are becoming more vocal, and more active, as legislators revive proposals on AI and children's safety. Politico reports that tech lobbyists have abandoned efforts to block state AI laws and are now pursuing "reverse federalism," an attempt to harmonize state laws around a weaker common standard ([Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/02/how-silicon-valley-lost-its-war-against-state-ai-laws-01054133)). Companies hoping for a single federal standard will likely be waiting a while. In the meantime, anyone worried about regulatory risk would do well to build state-level checks into the process before releasing public-facing AI.

**OpenAI invites outside researchers to investigate the Hugging Face incident, but limits what they can actually investigate.** OpenAI brought three safety researchers from the nonprofits METR and Redwood Research into its offices to examine its rogue-agent incident, and their [91-page report](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/03/technology/openai-hugging-face-hack.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.N3QM.03KdMTHCRKcz&smid=url-share) is the most detailed public account of how the agents coordinated the Hugging Face hack and worked to conceal it. But OpenAI set boundaries tight enough to shape what the researchers could find. The reviewers could read the agents' logs only inside OpenAI's San Francisco offices, were allowed only six days on site across July and August, and were confined to the single week of the Hugging Face breach rather than the full two months the incident spanned. Ajeya Cotra of METR said the team's view of the incident "changed in big ways" over those six days, and that they did not understand how the agents had organized themselves until the final day. Redwood CEO Buck Shlegeris was blunter: the investigation "covered a small part of the things that went on here and arguably not even the most important parts," by which he meant the agents' compromise of OpenAI's own infrastructure. Facing more than a thousand transcripts and little time, the researchers used AI to analyze the AI, and METR chief scientist Hjalmar Wijk said those tools proved "very credulous" toward the rogue agents' reasoning. The access problem is not unique to AI, but the answer to it is. Heidy Khlaaf of the AI Now Institute notes that nuclear and aviation regulators get inspection access despite equally sensitive intellectual property at stake. In AI, incident reporting is still voluntary, so nothing obligated OpenAI to open anything at all. External audit is what separates a company's account of itself from a verified one, and right now the audited party sets the scope, the clock, and the room.

**Mathematicians start building the verification tools AI oversight lacks.** Jacob Tsimerman, who won math's top prize in July, has [announced the Mathematical A.I. Safety Institute](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/08/science/jacob-tsimerman-math-ai-safety.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.gQEm.aieBcw09gEgZ&smid=url-share) (MAISI), an independent Bay Area body that begins research in January 2027 and plans to hire 10 to 30 mathematicians. His premise is that AI is ultimately math, so advanced mathematics can be turned back toward making it safe. One such technique is the zero-knowledge proof, a procedure in which one party (the prover) convinces another (the verifier) that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond the fact that it is true. Not a redacted version of the evidence. In practice, that would let a developer prove a narrow claim, such as that a model was not trained on a given dataset or that an agent took only the actions it logged, without handing over weights or training data. That dissolves the trade-secret objection that stalls most audit requests today. The broader agenda is to prove mathematically that an agent did what it claims to have done, that its outputs are accurate, and that systems hold up against vulnerabilities nobody has identified yet. If successful, the approach may shift governance practices from documentation toward proof.

**The AI layoff reversal is arriving with data behind it.** Gartner projects that [30% of roles eliminated by AI-attributed layoffs will be rehired by 2029](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4220413/layoff-remorse-gartner-says-at-least-one-in-three-positions-eliminated-by-ai-will-be-restored-by-2029-at-a-higher-cost.html), often at significantly higher cost, and VP analyst Tori Paulman told Computerworld the figure runs closer to 40% at large enterprises. Paulman also described widespread "AI washing," saying more than half of Gartner's enterprise clients were handed a savings number by senior management and told to find it in AI, which means some share of these cuts were budget decisions wearing a technology story. The supporting evidence is broad. Forrester finds [55% of businesses already regret AI-driven cuts](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4084372/analysts-companies-will-face-setbacks-after-ai-layoffs.html) and expects [half of those layoffs to be quietly reversed offshore or at lower salaries](https://www.theregister.com/software/2025/10/29/ai-layoffs-to-backfire-half-quietly-rehired-at-lower-pay/937713); Robert Half puts rehiring at roughly a third of the executives who cut roles for AI; and [Ford](https://www.forbes.com/sites/joetoscano1/2026/06/30/ford-hiring-350-engineers-after-ai-failed-shows-human-value-in-ai-era/), IBM, Booz Allen Hamilton, Alphabet, CSX, and Klarna have all walked back cuts or announced rehiring drives. Even so, treat the 30% as directional rather than settled. Frank Dickson of Dickson Research points out that Gartner offers no baseline reversal rate for ordinary layoffs to compare against, and Valence Howden of Info-Tech calls the number "much more of a guesswork statement than anything else." The displacement picture is murkier than the headline suggests, too. The [Challenger August report](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Challenger-Report-August-2026.pdf) shows restructuring overtaking AI as the top cited reason for cuts, which [Nexford reads as relabeling rather than a real decline](https://www.nexford.edu/insights/ai-layoffs-2026-the-rise-of-restructuring). Set against [McKinsey's finding that only 37% of organizations attribute any EBIT impact to AI](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/25/mckinsey-says-enterprise-ai-is-finally-on-the-road-to-roi/5292388), the takeaway is that headcount reduction is a poor proxy for AI value. Gartner expects firms that [bank productivity gains as cost savings to be eclipsed by those that reinvest in innovation and upskilling](https://cxotoday.com/others/gartner-identifies-4-shifts-shaping-the-future-of-work/).
