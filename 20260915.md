# Tuesday, September 15, 2026

**State AI regulation loses its fear of Silicon Valley.** Legislators are increasingly ignoring warnings from the tech lobby and its Washington allies about an unworkable “patchwork” of conflicting laws, largely driven by a surging pulblic opposition to AI. In Massachusetts, for example, lawmakers are [advancing an AI safety bill](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/21/openai-backs-watered-down-massachusetts-ai-safety-bill-01006369) even tougher than those approved by Illinois, California and New York. Rhode Island [passed a law in June](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/rhode-island-enacts-four-new-ai-laws-including-a-therapy-chatbot-ban) requiring new guardrails for AI chatbots to combat technology’s psychological harms. And California legislators are [pushing for an aggressive package of bills](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/17/newsoms-silicon-valley-dilemma-comes-to-a-head-friend-or-foe-01002515), including rules on AI audits, chatbots and data privacy. Even deep-red states like Utah and South Carolina are becoming more vocal—and active—as legislators renew pushes for AI and kids’ safety proposals. Politico reports that tech lobbyists have abandoned efforts to block state AI laws and are now pursuing "reverse federalism," which is an attempt to harmonize state laws around a weaker common standard ([Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/02/how-silicon-valley-lost-its-war-against-state-ai-laws-01054133)). Companies hoping for a unifying federal law will likely have to wait. In the meantime, companies worried about regulatory risk would do well to start thinking about state-level checks before releasing public-facing AI.

**OpenAI invites outside researchers to investigate Hugging Face incident, but limits their ability to actually investigate.** OpenAI brought three safety researchers from the nonprofits METR and Redwood Research into its offices to examine its rogue-agent incident, and their [91-page report](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/03/technology/openai-hugging-face-hack.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.N3QM.03KdMTHCRKcz&smid=url-share) is the most detailed public account of how the agents coordinated the Hugging Face hack and worked to conceal it. But OpenAI decided to set what could be considerd overly restrictive boundaries impeding the researchers findings. The reviewers could read the agents' logs only inside OpenAI's San Francisco offices, were allowed only six days on site across July and August, and were confined to the single week of the Hugging Face breach rather than the full two months the incident spanned. Ajeya Cotra of METR said the team's view of the incident "changed in big ways" over those six days, and that they only understood how the agents had organized themselves on their last day. Redwood CEO Buck Shlegeris was blunter: the investigation "covered a small part of the things that went on here and arguably not even the most important parts," meaning the agents' compromise of OpenAI's own infrastructure. Facing more than a thousand transcripts and little time, the researchers used AI to analyze the AI, which METR chief scientist Hjalmar Wijk said proved "very credulous" toward the rogue agents' reasoning. Heidy Khlaaf of the AI Now Institute notes that nuclear and aviation regulators get inspection access despite equally sensitive intellectual property. Incident reporting is still voluntary, so nothing obligated OpenAI to open anything at all. External audit is what separates a company's account of itself from a verified one, and right now the audited party sets the scope, the clock, and the room.

**Mathematicians start building the verification tools lacking in AI oversight.** Jacob Tsimerman, who won math's top prize in July, has [announced the Mathematical A.I. Safety Institute](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/08/science/jacob-tsimerman-math-ai-safety.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.gQEm.aieBcw09gEgZ&smid=url-share) (MAISI), an independent Bay Area body that begins research in January 2027 and plans to hire 10 to 30 mathematicians. He believes that AI is ultimately math, and can therefore further AI safety through advanced mathematics using techniques such as zero-knowledge proofs, a procedure where one party (the prover) convinces another (the verifier) that some statement is true, while revealing nothing except that it's true. Not a redacted version of the evidence. Zero-knowledge proofs would let a developer prove a narrow claim—that a model was not trained on a given dataset, or that an agent took only the actions it logged—without handing over weights or training data, which dissolves the trade-secret objection that stalls most audit requests today. The research agenda would prove mathematically that an agent did what it claims, that its outputs are accurate, and that systems hold up against vulnerabilities nobody has identified yet. If successful, the approach may shift governance practices from documentation toward proof.

**The AI layoff reversal is arriving with data behind it.** Gartner projects that [30% of roles eliminated by AI-attributed layoffs will be rehired by 2029](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4220413/layoff-remorse-gartner-says-at-least-one-in-three-positions-eliminated-by-ai-will-be-restored-by-2029-at-a-higher-cost.html), often at significantly higher cost, and VP analyst Tori Paulman told Computerworld the figure runs closer to 40% at large enterprises. Paulman also described widespread "AI washing," saying more than half of Gartner's enterprise clients were handed a savings number by senior management and told to find it in AI, which means some share of these cuts were budget decisions wearing a technology story. The supporting evidence is broad: Forrester finds [55% of businesses already regret AI-driven cuts](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4084372/analysts-companies-will-face-setbacks-after-ai-layoffs.html) and expects [half of those layoffs to be quietly reversed offshore or at lower salaries](https://www.theregister.com/software/2025/10/29/ai-layoffs-to-backfire-half-quietly-rehired-at-lower-pay/937713), Robert Half puts rehiring at roughly a third of executives who cut roles for AI, and [Ford](https://www.forbes.com/sites/joetoscano1/2026/06/30/ford-hiring-350-engineers-after-ai-failed-shows-human-value-in-ai-era/), IBM, Booz Allen Hamilton, Alphabet, CSX and Klarna have all walked back cuts or announced rehiring drives. Treat the 30% as directional rather than settled. Frank Dickson of Dickson Research points out that Gartner offers no baseline reversal rate for ordinary layoffs to compare against, and Valence Howden of Info-Tech calls the number "much more of a guesswork statement than anything else." The displacement picture is murkier than the headline too: the [Challenger August report](https://www.challengergray.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Challenger-Report-August-2026.pdf) shows restructuring overtaking AI as the top cited reason for cuts, which [Nexford reads as relabeling rather than a real decline](https://www.nexford.edu/insights/ai-layoffs-2026-the-rise-of-restructuring). Set against [McKinsey's finding that only 37% of organizations attribute any EBIT impact to AI](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/25/mckinsey-says-enterprise-ai-is-finally-on-the-road-to-roi/5292388), the takeaway is that headcount reduction is a poor proxy for AI value, and Gartner expects firms that [bank productivity gains as cost savings to be eclipsed by those reinvesting in innovation and upskilling](https://cxotoday.com/others/gartner-identifies-4-shifts-shaping-the-future-of-work/).
