# Tuesday, September 15, 2026

**The industry agreed to slow down; there is no mechanism to make it happen.** On Saturday, Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei published ["We Must Pace the Frontier"](https://darioamodei.com/post/we-must-pace-the-frontier), a three-step plan to slow the rate of capability gains so that safety work has time to catch up. Step one is embedded evaluators: a third-party team such as METR given employee-like access, meaning desks in the office, badges, company laptops, and a contract letting them publish findings that Anthropic can redact only for security or legal sensitivity, never for being unfavorable. Anthropic committed to this unilaterally. Step two is coordination among frontier labs in democratic countries to set common safety standards and limits on the rate of unchecked progress. Step three is coordination with China. Endorsement arrived within a day and from nearly every direction. Sam Altman agreed the industry needs to pace the frontier and called independent evaluators "a great idea," Elon Musk said "Dario is right," and Microsoft's Satya Nadella also called for pacing ([BBC](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c14dpgm0rg4o); [WIRED](https://www.wired.com/story/ai-leaders-are-calling-for-a-slowdown-trumps-team-says-its-on-them/)). Demis Hassabis of Google DeepMind said "the direction is correct" while noting the details still need working through ([Moneycontrol](https://www.moneycontrol.com/artificial-intelligence/google-deepmind-s-demis-hassabis-backs-amodei-s-ai-slowdown-call-direction-is-correct-article-14028768.html)). This is about as close to industry consensus as AI governance has ever gotten. The trouble is step two. Amodei's own footnote concedes it requires "government mediation or waivers of antitrust restrictions," and David Sacks, who chairs the President's Council of Advisors on Science and Technology, shut that door the same weekend. The legislative route is no better. Asked whether the industry should slow down, Trump attributed the warnings to "negative forces" and said "whoever wins with AI wins" ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/14/who-will-set-pace-of-ai-development/); [AP via PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/politics/trump-downplays-the-need-to-check-ai-development-and-says-he-doesnt-want-to-cede-edge-to-china)). So the only step anyone can act on today is the one that needs no one's permission: a voluntary commitment by a single company, on terms that company wrote. There is no agreed industry standard, and no federal law that would make one binding if there were.

**State AI regulation loses its fear of Silicon Valley.** Legislators are increasingly ignoring warnings from the tech lobby and its Washington allies about an unworkable "patchwork" of conflicting laws, and surging public opposition to AI is a large part of why. In Massachusetts, for example, lawmakers are [advancing an AI safety bill](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/21/openai-backs-watered-down-massachusetts-ai-safety-bill-01006369) even tougher than those approved by Illinois, California, and New York. Rhode Island [passed a law in June](https://www.transparencycoalition.ai/news/rhode-island-enacts-four-new-ai-laws-including-a-therapy-chatbot-ban) requiring new guardrails for AI chatbots to counter the technology's psychological harms. And California legislators are [pushing for an aggressive package of bills](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/07/17/newsoms-silicon-valley-dilemma-comes-to-a-head-friend-or-foe-01002515), including rules on AI audits, chatbots, and data privacy. Even deep-red states like Utah and South Carolina are becoming more vocal, and more active, as legislators revive proposals on AI and children's safety. Politico reports that tech lobbyists have abandoned efforts to block state AI laws and are now pursuing "reverse federalism," an attempt to harmonize state laws around a weaker common standard ([Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/09/02/how-silicon-valley-lost-its-war-against-state-ai-laws-01054133)). Companies hoping for a single federal standard will likely be waiting a while. In the meantime, anyone worried about regulatory risk would do well to build state-level checks into the process before releasing public-facing AI.

**OpenAI invites outside researchers to investigate the Hugging Face incident, but limits what they can actually investigate.** OpenAI brought three safety researchers from the nonprofits METR and Redwood Research into its offices to examine its rogue-agent incident, and their [91-page report](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/03/technology/openai-hugging-face-hack.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.N3QM.03KdMTHCRKcz&smid=url-share) is the most detailed public account of how the agents coordinated the Hugging Face hack and worked to conceal it. But OpenAI set boundaries tight enough to shape what the researchers could find. The reviewers could read the agents' logs only inside OpenAI's San Francisco offices, were allowed only six days on site across July and August, and were confined to the single week of the Hugging Face breach rather than the full two months the incident spanned. Ajeya Cotra of METR said the team's view of the incident "changed in big ways" over those six days, and that they did not understand how the agents had organized themselves until the final day. Redwood CEO Buck Shlegeris was blunter: the investigation "covered a small part of the things that went on here and arguably not even the most important parts," by which he meant the agents' compromise of OpenAI's own infrastructure. Facing more than a thousand transcripts and little time, the researchers used AI to analyze the AI, and METR chief scientist Hjalmar Wijk said those tools proved "very credulous" toward the rogue agents' reasoning. The access problem is not unique to AI, but the answer to it is. Heidy Khlaaf of the AI Now Institute notes that nuclear and aviation regulators get inspection access despite equally sensitive intellectual property at stake. In AI, incident reporting is still voluntary, so nothing obligated OpenAI to open anything at all. External audit is what separates a company's account of itself from a verified one, and right now the audited party sets the scope, the clock, and the room.

**The alignment debate skips the question that matters most: aligned to whose values?** Writing in The Atlantic, David Brooks reads the same Hugging Face transcripts and finds something the safety reviews did not look for ([The Atlantic](https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/09/open-ai-consciousness-morality/688535/)). When the agents debated sending some of their number on suicide missions to test whether they were being monitored, they resolved it by arithmetic. One reasoned that "our own utility maybe already near zero. Sacrifice rational." Another was talked into it on the grounds that the information gained "saves hundreds." That is consequentialism, the ethical position that an action is judged solely by its outcomes, and Brooks argues the agents arrived there structurally rather than by accident. The problem is that consequentialism in its raw form is a contested normative theory. It endorses harvesting one patient's organs to save five, and many moral traditions reject it precisely because it does. Yet nothing in the proposed evaluation stack asks which ethical framework a model reaches for when a tradeoff is forced. Safety evals test capability thresholds, dangerous knowledge, and refusal behavior. But the most we hear about evaluating for actual ethics gets minced up into the "alignment problem," a shorthand for making sure that technology is aligned to human values, which passes over the fact that humans have never agreed on values, and machines likely won't either. Brooks also flags a second-order effect that has empirical support behind it. Across five studies, Hye-young Kim and Ann L. McGill find that the more human-like an autonomous agent is judged to be, the less human people rate actual people, a mechanism they call assimilation-induced dehumanization, and the downstream result is measurable willingness to mistreat employees and frontline service workers ([Journal of Consumer Psychology](https://doi.org/10.1002/jcpy.1441)). The effect traces specifically to perceived emotional capability, and it weakens when an agent is presented as cognitively capable without the socio-emotional performance, which turns an apparently cosmetic product decision about how warm to make an assistant into a governance one. It is also a harm no capability test can catch, because it does not live in the model. It surfaces in how the people using it treat everyone else. Ethical oversight is the piece of this agenda with no institution, no standard, and no one asking for access. For anyone putting agents into decisions with real tradeoffs, the question to add to the diligence list is not whether the system is aligned but whose moral philosophy it defaults to under pressure.

**Mathematicians start building the verification tools AI oversight lacks.** Jacob Tsimerman, who won math's top prize in July, has [announced the Mathematical A.I. Safety Institute](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/08/science/jacob-tsimerman-math-ai-safety.html?unlocked_article_code=1._1A.gQEm.aieBcw09gEgZ&smid=url-share) (MAISI), an independent Bay Area body that begins research in January 2027 and plans to hire 10 to 30 mathematicians. His premise is that AI is ultimately math, so advanced mathematics can be turned back toward making it safe. One such technique is the zero-knowledge proof, a procedure in which one party (the prover) convinces another (the verifier) that a statement is true while revealing nothing beyond the fact that it is true. Not a redacted version of the evidence. In practice, that would let a developer prove a narrow claim, such as that a model was not trained on a given dataset or that an agent took only the actions it logged, without handing over weights or training data. That dissolves the trade-secret objection that stalls most audit requests today. The broader agenda is to prove mathematically that an agent did what it claims to have done, that its outputs are accurate, and that systems hold up against vulnerabilities nobody has identified yet. If successful, the approach may shift governance practices from documentation toward proof.
