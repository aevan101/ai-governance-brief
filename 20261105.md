# AI Governance Brief

Thursday, November 5, 2026

**The big takeaway: Write policy that can be enforced through architecture.** Rules that once lived in acceptable-use documents and steering committee decks are now written as configurations that gateways and agent frameworks enforce on every request. For example, a token budget that halts an API call or an 'allow list' that prevents an unverified foundation model from being used. This is what is often referred to as "policy as code," meaning the rule runs in the system and does not depend on someone remembering to apply it. Four questions for any AI deployment:

1. What is the spend cap?
2. How is it enforced?
3. Who can change it?
4. How do we audit it?

**Spend caps are now a gateway feature, not a finance-team spreadsheet.** Oracle and Fortinet’s FortiAIGate architecture enforces spend caps simultaneously at the org, workspace, project, user, and virtual-key levels, and requests halt when any cap is exhausted. The same gateway adds data loss prevention (DLP) covering 35 sensitive data categories, with block-or-redact enforcement, plus role-based approval requirements for policy changes and full audit logs of prompts, outputs, and tool calls ([Cybersecurity News](https://cybersecuritynews.com/fortinet-brings-ai-agent-security-to-oracle-cloud/)). Microsoft’s Azure API Management (APIM) takes a similar approach with the `llm-token-limit` policy in its AI gateway capabilities ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/api-management/llm-token-limit-policy)). One policy statement sets a tokens-per-minute rate limit, a token quota over an hourly, daily, weekly, monthly, or yearly window, or both. Limits attach to a counter key, such as a subscription key, an IP address, or a custom expression, and can be applied at global, workspace, product, API, or operation scope. A caller who exceeds the rate limit receives a 429 response, and one who exceeds the quota receives a 403. The policy can estimate prompt tokens in advance so oversized requests never reach the model, and a companion policy emits per-consumer token metrics to Application Insights, with prompts and completions logged to Azure Monitor ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/api-management/genai-gateway-capabilities)). Both treat the budget as a rule enforced in the request path, not a report reviewed after the bill arrives.

**Banks are applying the same logic to agents: block at the perimeter, bound permissions inside.** RBC, TD, BMO, and CIBC have blocked third-party personal AI agents from accessing customer accounts, citing fraud risk; BMO’s block is active now ([CBC News](https://www.cbc.ca/news/business/canadian-banks-block-ai-9.7400777), [Globe and Mail](https://www.theglobeandmail.com/business/article-bmo-blocks-third-party-ai-agents-rbc-muse-october-28)). Bank of America has warned customers against sharing passwords with AI agents without implementing a technical block ([ZDNET](https://www.zdnet.com/innovation/bank-of-america-customers-no-sharing-passwords-ai-agents)). The underlying problem is liability. When an agent acts on behalf of a customer, responsibility for fraud is unresolved, and banks are opting to block rather than build authentication frameworks. Barclays shows what building looks like. Its Principal AI Engineer Andy McMahon described the bank’s agent control framework publicly: telemetry, logs, and traces for observability; permission boundaries restricting tool access by task; programmatic kill switches; and a sequencing policy requiring internal deployment before any customer-facing deployment. In his words: “You can’t just give an AI agent carte blanche to do what it wants. You can’t give it access to all of your tools, all of your systems.” ([QA Financial](https://qa-financial.com/barclays-names-ai-chief-as-testing-challenges-grow)) It is one of the more detailed public descriptions of an agent permission architecture from a major bank. Like spend caps, each of these controls is enforced by the system, not left to the judgment of the agent or the user.

**Google is rolling agents out to companies before consumers because enterprises demand controls.** Google has yet to launch a dedicated AI agent app for consumers. Instead, it launched an agent for businesses early this month, pitching it as an all-purpose tool that answers questions, manages work tasks, and writes code. Agents are risky, as recent agent-driven cyberattacks show, and largely untested at mass scale. Starting with a narrower group of sophisticated users gives Google feedback before it reaches a larger, less controlled audience. CEO Sundar Pichai told analysts that the company rolled out a Gemini agent to companies first “because they have the highest bar for security, governance and controls,” and that Google would bring that experience to consumers later this year, starting with the billion users of its Gemini app ([WSJ](https://www.wsj.com/tech/ai/alphabet-still-has-the-ingredients-to-win-in-an-ai-pullback-6583aa7c?st=sEQnm1&reflink=desktopwebshare_permalink)). The controls described above, including spend caps, permission boundaries, and audit logs, are what that bar looks like in practice.

**Governance structure is now the strongest predictor of AI ROI by a wide margin.** The Wharton/GBK 2026 AI Adoption Report (n=1,416) finds 58% of organizations with a dedicated chief AI officer (CAIO) report strong ROI, versus 8% with no AI leadership role, a 7x gap ([Wharton/GBK](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/special-report/2026-ai-adoption-report)). Platform standardization produces a similar split: 50% strong ROI when standardized on a single approved tool versus 9% still experimenting without a defined approach. The CAIO effect is weaker when the title is reassigned to an existing executive (24% strongly positive) than when it goes to a genuine new hire (58%), and two-thirds of this year’s CAIO growth came from reassignments. DDI’s Global Leadership Forecast 2027 points the same way: only 18% of leaders check AI output quality, yet 75% claim quality has improved, and only 22% operate under a clear AI policy ([DDI](https://www.ddi.com/research/global-leadership-forecast-2027/ai-readiness)). Together, the two datasets make the case for treating governance advisory as a prerequisite for ROI, not an add-on. They also expose the weakness of governance that relies on people. A policy that few leaders follow and a quality check that few leaders perform protect no one.

**Outside the biggest tech firms, there is still no measurable AI payoff.** Torsten Slok, a partner at Apollo Global Management, finds that revenue per employee at the “Magnificent 7” has risen about 30% over four years, while the rest of the S&P 500 has been flat, and that profit margins show the same split over an 11-year window ([MSN](https://www.msn.com/en-us/money/general/this-economist-sees-no-signs-of-ai-payoff-beyond-the-major-tech-firms/ar-AA2gerM9), citing Bloomberg). The tech giants sell AI as well as use it, so Slok looks for gains in sectors that are investing in AI tools and does not find them. Total factor productivity, which compares an industry’s total output to the inputs used to produce it, spiked in early 2023, possibly from early adoption in fields like programming, and has since fallen to zero. His reading is that mainstream corporate America now treats AI as a cost that weighs on profits, not a driver of them. He argues that cheaper models are unlikely to change this because “AI does not understand accountability, trust, culture, unwritten rules, ethical trade-offs, or fairness,” all of which shape reputational risk and ultimately revenue and margins. His bottom line: “The AI story will be decided in the S&P 493, not the Mag 7.” 

**Some companies that rushed into AI are now pulling back, and the cleanup is landing on workers.** Meta scaled back plans to cut staff and deploy more AI agents after employees spent growing amounts of time fixing technical and security errors, and it is rebuilding the managerial ranks it thinned in 2023 ([The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/13/ai-companies-rushed-workers-fallout)). Training is a major gap: an Indeed survey finds more than half of employees are not getting the AI training they need, and only 45% of managers feel equipped to lead “AI natives.” Microsoft strategy chief Kathleen Hogan summed up the problem: “access and usage do not equal transformation,” and “adding agents to a broken process still leaves a broken process.” The legal risk is even more daunting. An expert witness for 3M apparently relied on ChatGPT for a report related to a ++[2020 explosion at Watson Grinding](https://www.houstonpublicmedia.org/articles/news/energy-environment/2023/06/30/455871/houston-explosion-2020-watson-grinding-federal-investigation/?ref=404media.co)++, which a jury ordered 3M to pay $61.5 million. 

**Anthropic formally endorses court-ordered model blocks and embedded auditors as governance mechanisms.** Reuters reported on October 28 that an October 15 Anthropic letter to Governor Newsom proposed three specific mechanisms California should codify ([Reuters / U.S. News](https://www.usnews.com/news/top-news/articles/2026-10-28/anthropic-tells-california-governor-courts-should-be-able-to-block-risky-ai-models)): independent safety evaluators physically embedded inside frontier AI companies and funded through a state pool (not by the company being examined); rapid shutdown capability at every leading developer; and a court-order mechanism allowing the California attorney general to petition to pause a model’s deployment or development when a near-term catastrophic risk is found. Anthropic also called for mandatory developer risk reports every six months ([The Next Web](https://thenextweb.com/news/anthropic-newsom-letter-courts-block-ai-models)). Anthropic backs rapid shutdown capability but warns that kill switches alone are not enough. It told Newsom they could provide a “false sense of reassurance” and favors narrower steps, such as disabling a single agent or cutting off one customer’s access. This is the first time a major frontier lab has formally endorsed judicial pre-deployment blocks, setting a reference point for 2027 state legislation. None of this is enacted yet. The same caution applies inside the enterprise: an automated control is only as trustworthy as the process that sets and changes it.
