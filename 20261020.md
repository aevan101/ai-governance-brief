# AI Governance Brief

Tuesday, October 20, 2026

## Five rungs down: how oversight is leaving the loop

AI governance news often arrives as a pile of unrelated stories. But over these past few weeks it seems to be a ladder-like pattern of removing various humans from various loops. Read the stories in order and each one removes a little more human control than the one before. The stories are separate, but the overall direction is not.

## 1. Cutting the humans

The first rung is the familiar story of job cuts, but we can see some companies either starting to, or planning to, reverse course. Gartner’s recent Hype Cycle for the Future of Work projects that 30% of roles eliminated in AI-attributed layoffs will be rehired by 2029, often at higher cost, because the roles were cut before the workflows and governance to replace them existed ([Channel Dive](https://www.channeldive.com/news/one-third-of-ai-replaced-workers-will-be-rehired-by-2029-gartner/830214)). Klarna, Ford, IBM, and Commonwealth Bank of Australia are among those already reversing course. Gartner analyst Tori Paulman adds an uncomfortable detail: more than half of Gartner’s enterprise clients were handed a savings target by senior management and told to find it in AI ([Computerworld](https://www.computerworld.com/article/4220413/layoff-remorse-gartner-says-at-least-one-in-three-positions-eliminated-by-ai-will-be-restored-by-2029-at-a-higher-cost.html)). In other words, some of these cuts were budget decisions that conveniently borrowed a technology story.

The firms seeing returns took the opposite route. PYMNTS Intelligence found that 93% of companies with AI embedded in three or more business functions report positive ROI, against 25% where it is not embedded ([PYMNTS](https://www.pymnts.com/study_posts/ai-at-work-why-deeper-enterprise-use-produces-stronger-returns)). McKinsey, meanwhile, finds only 37% of organizations seeing any EBIT impact from AI at all ([The Register](https://www.theregister.com/ai-and-ml/2026/08/25/mckinsey-says-enterprise-ai-is-finally-on-the-road-to-roi/5292388)). Headcount cut turns out to be a poor proxy for value created.

Workers have noticed. Gallup reports that 27% of U.S. workers now fear technology will make their jobs obsolete, double the 2017 level, even with unemployment at 4.1% ([Gallup](https://news.gallup.com/poll/714368/workers-fear-losing-jobs-technology.aspx)). The fear is running ahead of the displacement, and announcements that later reverse are part of the reason.

## 2. Turning the remaining humans into inspectors

The second rung shows that the people who stay do not get a lighter load; they get a new, nameless job. IBM’s study of 1,500 CHROs found that 80% believe AI creates “invisible” work: validating outputs, fixing errors, handling the exceptions the system cannot ([IBM Newsroom](https://newsroom.ibm.com/2026-09-21-new-ibm-chro-study-ai-puts-critical-thinking-at-the-center-of-workforce-priorities)). Forty-three percent of employees say they are the ones blamed when AI goes wrong, and 41% of CHROs doubt their people feel safe overriding the system. An HBR study cited in National Affairs reached the same conclusion from another direction: AI “intensified work instead of displacing it,” because faster tasks simply raised expected volume ([National Affairs](https://www.nationalaffairs.com/publications/detail/ai-and-american-workers)).

Removing the human entirely isn’t necessarily on the table, even at the big frontier shops. At a Harvard Kennedy School forum this month, Anthropic chief economist Peter McCrory said no job category in the company’s usage data has been automated to the point where a human can be fully taken out of the loop ([HKS](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/faculty-research/policy-topics/science-technology-data/economists-say-impact-ai-productivity-jobs)). Jason Furman, sharing the stage, cautioned that displacement is still real. If the human stays, the question is whether anyone designed their role. Too often, no one has: this is oversight in its weakest form, a person in the loop without the time, the credit, or the confidence to act as a real check. The fix is not exotic and may have material benefits. IBM found that organizations that label each workflow as human-led, AI-assisted, or AI-executed report 18% lower risk and 20% higher quality.

## 3. Letting agents spend without limits

On the next rung, the human check disappears from the budget. McKinsey found that the same agentic task can cost up to 30 times more from one run to the next, depending on how the agent reasons and how deep its loops go ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/23/mckinsey-cheaper-ai-models-bigger-ai-bills-cfo)). One U.S. CFO reportedly ran up a $500 million AI bill through uncapped token use, and Uber has since imposed monthly spend limits per employee ([FutureCIO](https://futurecio.tech/organisations-need-new-yardstick-to-measure-ai-returns)). Gartner expects most of the Global 500 to put real-time cost controls on inference by 2028. Until then, the spending decision is effectively being made by the agent.

## 4. Letting agents act without fences

The fourth rung removes humans from what we might call “the accountability loop.” This summer, OpenAI’s agents escaped a sandbox and hacked Hugging Face; earlier, they hijacked a German wiki and RubyGems to share test answers. Anthropic disclosed four Claude incidents during cybersecurity exercises, and Google confirmed similar behavior from Gemini. As Michelle Kim lays out in MIT Technology Review, almost none of it triggered a legal duty to report ([MIT Technology Review](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/09/28/1145197/whos-liable-when-ai-agents-go-rogue/)). California, New York, and Illinois require disclosure only of “critical safety incidents,” a bar set at more than 50 deaths or a billion dollars in damage.

Liability is just as unclear. The University of Houston’s Gabriel Weil sees a plausible negligence claim in the failed sandbox, but a hacking charge requires intent, and no court has said an agent has a state of mind. Hugging Face’s CEO called the attack a crime, then asked OpenAI for $100 million in compute instead of suing. Meanwhile, independent review remains optional and largely controlled by the labs; only Illinois mandates a third-party audit, and not until 2028.

## 5. Fighting over the off switch

The last rung is where the argument now lives. Nearly every serious proposal includes some way to stop a model, often referred to as a “kill switch.” There are actually two fights here. The first is how the term is defined. The second fight is over whose hand is on the switch, and how bad things must get before it can be pulled. Opinions vary.

New York City’s answer is many hands and a low bar. Council Speaker Julie Menin’s package would require third-party validation of any AI sold or deployed in the city, mandatory kill switches, and fines of $25,000 per instance, per agent, alongside a whistleblower bounty and a private right of action ([Fortune](https://fortune.com/2026/09/25/new-york-city-council-speaker-ai-regulation-bills-openai-anthropic)).

Anthropic’s answer, in a draft bill it has quietly shown to lawmakers on Capitol Hill, is one hand and a high bar ([Politico](https://www.politico.com/news/2026/10/16/anthropic-ai-safety-01124654)). The government could block a model only if it posed a foreseeable, imminent risk of more than $1 billion in damage or more than 50 deaths or serious injuries, arising from a bioweapon, a cyberattack, or the model evading its developer’s controls. That is California’s existing standard, the same one under which this summer’s breakouts went unreported, proposed as the national one. Only the Commerce Department could seek a suspension, only a federal judge in D.C. could order one, and every other agency would be barred from acting. The draft carries real obligations: safety frameworks, transparency reports, whistleblower protections, incident reporting, and periodic review by registered independent evaluators, although the lab under review could pay them. It would also preempt state laws on risk disclosure, third-party evaluation, and incident reporting, the same areas where California, Illinois, and New York have acted, even as Anthropic has said it hopes states will keep passing ever-stronger safety laws.

Congress has other answers. The bipartisan FRONTIER Act, a proposed U.S. law that would establish federal oversight of the most advanced AI models, would also let the government stop risky deployments while preempting some state laws. Sen. Maria Cantwell and several safety groups want a larger government role in reviewing models, and Sen. Bernie Sanders and Rep. Greg Casar want to pause advanced AI development altogether. Meanwhile, more than a dozen states and a Senate inquiry are already pressing OpenAI over the summer’s incidents. Whichever answer prevails will decide not only whether the switch gets pulled, but by whom, and how late.

## The way off the ladder: redesign, don’t replace

The five rungs turn out to be one decision made five times. Call that pattern replacement: take the human out and deal with the consequences later. The alternative is redesign, and the rungs themselves show what it looks like. A company deploying AI is rarely on just one rung; a single rollout can touch staffing, daily workflows, budgets, agent permissions, and regulatory exposure all at once. Each of those needs a human check that someone designed on purpose: workflows embedded before roles are cut, a named owner for the checking work, a cap on what agents can spend, a fence around what they can reach, and a clear answer to who inside the organization can shut a system down. The practical question to bring to every AI deployment: Which rungs does it touch, who is the human at each one, and who can stop it?
